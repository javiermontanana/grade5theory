More on triplets and duplets

In the previous section, we saw examples of triplets and duplets where they were used to divide a single beat into 2 or 3 parts.

But this is not the only use of triplets and duplets. Remember the general meaning:

  • a triplet fits 3 in the space of 2.
  • a duplet fits 2 in the space of 3.

We can use this definition and have triplets or duplets with any note values. For example, a 2/4 bar is 2 crotchets long. I could have a triplet of crotchets, allowing me to fit 3 crotchets into a bar

Other Tuplets

Triplets and duplets are just the two simplest kinds of tuplets. Tuplets are any division of a note value which allows us to fit a certain number of notes in the space that would normally correspond to another number.

Other commonly used tuplets are quintuplets (group of 5) and sextuplets (group of 6), which usually fit into the space of 4. Those are all the tuplets you need to know for the grade 5 theory exam, but there can be a tuplet for any number. In every case, what the tuplet means is that these however many notes of a certain value, should be played in the time that it would normally take to play some other number of notes of that value. In the case of the triplet, that other number is 2, and in the case of the duplet, that other number is 3. In the case of groups of more than 3, that other number will usually be clear from the broader context of the time signature and the other notes in the bar, or will sometimes be made clear by the composer.
