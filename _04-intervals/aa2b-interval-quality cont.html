In this section we will learn to fully identify all the intervals between any two notes from the C major scale.

Below are 2 octaves of the C major scale. As we already know, there are semitones E-F and B-C (these are highlighted) and all the other steps are tones. A step of a tone is bigger than a step of a semitone, and we will use this fact to work out the interval quality.

Let's go through all the intervals.

Seconds

We can see that all the seconds are tones, except for E-F and B-C which are semitones. As we know from the previous section, seconds can be major or minor. The tones are major, the semitones are minor.

The animation below shows for all the seconds, whether they are major or minor. As we move our two selected notes, we see that if the semitone falls inside the interval, it is minor. Otherwise it is major.

Thirds

We can see that some thirds are made up of two tones, and others are made up of a tone and a semitone. The ones with the semitone are smaller so they are minor. The others are major.

In the animation below, you can see that if the semitone falls between the two notes, then it is a minor third.

Fourths

Remember that fourths can't be major or minor. Instead, they are perfect.

Most fourths contain two tones and a semitone. Those are perfect. The only exception is F-B, which doesn't contain a semitone (it contains 3 tones) so it's augmented.

Fifths

Like fourths, fifths are also perfect rather than major or minor. Most fifths contain one semitone. The only exception is B-F, which contains two. Because it contains an one more semitone, it is smaller than the perfect ones, so it is diminished. This might seem confusing, but remember that when we say it contains one more semitone, we don't mean that the interval is a semitone bigger. We mean that one of the steps is a semitone rather than a tone, so the interval is actually smaller.

Sixths

Some sixths contain just one semitone and some contain two. The ones that contain two are smaller, so they are minor. The ones that only contain one are major (read the paragraph on fifths if you find this confusing).

Sevenths

Similar to sixths, sevenths are major if they contain one semitone, minor if they contain two.

Unisons and Octaves

All the unisons and all the octaves are the same size. All the unisons are between two of the same note, and all the octaves contain two semitones. They are all perfect (remember that unisons and octaves can't be major or minor).

Summary

Congratulations! You are now able to identify any interval between natural notes. This is a very important step, so make sure you understand everything and you give yourself enough practice before tackling intervals with sharps and flats.

In the next section, we will combine what we have learnt in this section and the previous one, and then you will be able to identify any interval.
