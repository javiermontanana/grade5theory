--- layout: chapter title: Scales 13 - Why are these notes raised in the minor scales? ---

In the previous section, we discussed how the tonic is the final arrival point of the music. This is achieved through the relationship between the dominant and the tonic. In particular, the dominant chord has need of resolution towards the tonic chord. You'll learn more about chords in a different chapter, but very briefly, the dominant chord is a set of notes which includes the 7th scale degree, and in order for the chord to function as a dominant, it must contain the leading note, so the 7th must be raised in minor keys. Chords are related to harmony, which is where the hamonic scale gets it's name.

The natural minor scale doesn't contain the raised leading note, so it does not by itself establish a key. In fact, the only forms of the minor scale you will see in the grade 5 theory exam are the melodic and harmonic minor, and these are probably the ones you're used to playing on your instrument.

So the need for the raised leading note explains why we raise the 7th scale degree in the harmonic scale. But what about the melodic scale? Why do we also raise the 6th scale degree, and why is it different when it descends?

Melodic comes from the word melody. A melody is a sequence of notes, a tune. In order to write melodies that have melodic fluency, we try to follow certain rules, and one of these rules is avoiding augmented intervals.

So let's say we have a melody that goes up the scale, from the dominant to the tonic.

We want the semitone between the leading note and the tonic, but if we just raise the leading note like in the harmonic scale, we have an augmented 2nd between scale degrees 6 and 7. So in order to avoid this augmented 2nd, we also raise scale degree 6. And that's how we obtain that part of the melodic scale.

But why is it different when we go the other way? When we move down from the tonic to the dominant,

we don't need to raise the leading note at all because we are moving away from the tonic. The leading note wants to resolve up to the tonic and when we go down the scale we are doing precisely the opposite, so the melodic motion downwards is facilitated by not raising the 7th degree. As we are not raising the 7th degree, we don't need to raise the 6th either, so we just use the same notes as in the natural scale.

This explanation for the melodic and harmonic scales is meant to help you remember them and understand them better. But you shouldn't take these as hard rules that mean that the melodic scale must be used for melodies or anything like that. In practice, composers use scales rather freely, and the reality is that, although we call these scales melodic and harmonic, it is not the case that one is always use for melodies and the other for harmony. What we can say is that, in minor keys, the 7th degree is often raised, and the 6th degree is sometimes raised, and often this avoids the augmented second between the 6th and 7th degrees.
