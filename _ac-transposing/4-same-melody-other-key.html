Of the three methods we've used in the previous section, the third is a little unrigorous. This is because we can't always describe a set of notes as scale degrees of a certain scale. In cases where the notes don't fit into a certain scale we will have to look purely at the intervals and use one of the other methods.

But in a lot of examples it is useful, and I have included it because it shows how transposing a melody can be seen as moving it to a deifferent key.

In the chapter on keys and scales we saw the example of happy birthday, and we showed the same melody in G major and in A major. Here it is again, but this time with the appropriate key signatures.

If you listen to these examples, they will sound like the same tune moved up or down by a step (major 2nd). Our G major example is a little lower, so it may be more comfortable to sing for someone with a slightly lower voice, and the A major example may be mor comfortable for someone with a slightly higher voice, but if these two people sing in their comfortable registers, they are still singing the same tune.

How is it that we have changed all the notes in the song, but it still sounds like the same song? This may seem very obvious to you, or it may seem very surprising. Either way, you should think about it, because it says something important about how we perceive music. It is because most of us don't identify notes in an of themselves. Instead, we identify the relationships between the notes (intervals).

For this reason, being able to transpose means you are able to understand what the same tune would look like if it's moved a bit higher or lower, sung by a different register voice, played by a different register instrument or moved to a different key. It is an essential part of being properly fluent with music.

In fact, this gives us a new understanding for many things. In the scales chapter, we constructed all the major scales. Now we can see that all we were doing was transpose one of the major scales through through many different intervals. likewise for the minor scales.
