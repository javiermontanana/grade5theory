--- layout: chapter title: Why are these notes raised in the minor scales? ---

In the previous section, we discussed how the tonic is the final point of resolution. This is achieved by the semitone motion from the leading note to the tonic. The natural minor scale doesn't contain this semitone, as it doesn't have the raised 7th degree, so it does not by itself establish a key. In fact, the only forms of the minor scale you will see in the grade 5 theory exam are the melodic and harmonic minor. These are the more commonly used ones and these are probably also the ones you're used to playing on your instrument.

Of particular importance for establishing the tonic as the note of the key is the relationship between the dominant chord and the tonic chord. You'll learn more about chords in a different chapter, but very briefly, the dominant chord is a set of notes which includes the 7th scale degree. In order for the chord to have a dominant function it must contain the raised 7th degree. The dominant function is precisely this tension and need of resolution towards to the tonic. This explains the raised 7th degree of the harmonic scale. The word "harmonic" refers to its connection to harmonic function—the relationships between chords.

But what about the melodic scale? Why do we also raise the 6th scale degree, and why is it different when it descends?

Just like harmonic refers to harmony, melodic refers to melody. A melody is a sequence of notes, or a tune. The melodic scale also creates this sense of pull towards the tonic, but avoids the interval of an augmented second that we find between scale degrees 6 and 7 in the harmonic scale. Augmented intervals can make a melody sound awkward and it used to be the best practice to try to avoid them, especially in vocal music, in order to make melodies more singable and smooth-flowing.

So let's say we have a melody that goes up the scale, from the dominant to the tonic:

We want to raise the 7th degree, in order to create this pull towards the tonic, but if we just raise the 7th degree, like in the harmonic scale, we have an augmented 2nd between scale degrees 6 and 7. So in order to avoid this augmented 2nd, we also raise scale degree 6.

But why is it different when we go down? When we move down from the tonic to the dominant, we don't need to raise the 7th degree because we are moving away from the tonic. The leading note wants to resolve up to the tonic and when we go down the scale we are doing precisely the opposite, so the melodic motion downwards is facilitated by not raising the 7th degree. As we are not raising the 7th degree, we don't need to raise the 6th either, so we just use the same notes as in the natural scale.

This explanation for the melodic and harmonic scales is meant to help you remember them and understand them better, but you shouldn't take these as hard rules dictating when one or another should be used. In practice, composers use scales rather freely, and the reality is that, although we call these scales melodic and harmonic, it is not the case that one is always used for melodies and the other for harmony. What we can generally say is that, in minor keys, the 7th degree is often raised, and the 6th degree is sometimes raised, and often this avoids the augmented second between the 6th and 7th degrees.
