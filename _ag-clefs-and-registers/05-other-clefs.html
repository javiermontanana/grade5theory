By understanding that each clef represents a certain pitch and placing it on a certain staff-line, you will be able to read notes in any clef without having to memorise where each note is.

You will even be able to read notes in clefs you've never seen before. In older editions and especially in manuscripts, you may find some more unusual clefs. Remeber that different clefs are used to centre the staff on different registers and avoid ledger lines. In handwritten music, too many ledger lines can get extremely annoying to write, as each one has to be drawn specially. Besides, if you write music very far above one staff, you can run into space issues with the staff above. With modern notation software, none of that is an issue. For this reason, musicians used to be trained to read music fluently in many more clefs than we are now.

Here are some examples of other clefs that we haven't seen before, but that you'll be able to work out from understanding the meaning of the clefs.

C clef on the first line (known as soprano clef) is used for soprano voices. Newer editions tend to use treble clef instead, but you may still see this fairly often.

G clef on the first line (known as french violin clef) was used fairly often in the baroque period for recorder parts and for an old instrument called piccolo violin, which was slightly higher in register than a regular violin. It was also use for violin parts by French composers (hence the name).
