In the previous section we learnt how we can use the staff to represent pitch. But with just pitch we can't write a melody. We can write a series of notes, one after another, but we can't yet write the rhythm of the melody. Rhythm relates to how sounds take place in time.

Some notes in a melody will be longer than others, and by their appearance, we can indicate how long they last. We call these different "types" of notes which show us the duration of the note different note values.

Note Values

As we learnt in the previous section, the note-head is the circle bit of the note and its position on the staf shows us the pitch.

The stem is the stick that comes out of the note-head in some values, and it can go up or down, it makes no difference.

Crotchet

A crotchet has a coloured-in note-head and a stem. For the purposes of describing the duration of the different note-values, let's say that a crotchet has a duration of 1. This will allow us to describe the other durations in terms of crotchets.

Minim

A minim has an empty note-head and a stem. It lasts for 2 crotchets.

Semibreve

A semibreve has an empty note-head. A semibreve lasts for 2 minims, or 4 crotchets.

Quaver

A quaver lasts for half a crotchet. A quaver has a coloured-in note-head, and a stem with a flag.

When we have two or more quavers in a row, we can connect them with a beam instead of using a flag for each one.

Rests

Each note value also has a corresponding rest. The rest means a silence with a duration of its value.

Bars and Time Signatures

Most music is organised into bars which have 2, 3, or 4 beats. The beat is a steady pulse that fits the music. If you clap along at a steady pulse, you will probably clap the beat.

The first beat of each bar feels stronger, so music that has 2 beats in a bar feels like it's going:
one, two, one, two ...

Music with three beats in a bar feels like it's going:
one, two, three, one, two, three ...

And music with 4 beats in a bar feels like it's going:
one, two, three, four, one, two, three, four ...

The time signature is a pair of numbers that we write at the beginning of the first line of a piece. It tells about the duration and number of beats in each bar.

Later you will learn about all the time signatures, but for now, I'd like you to know about 3 very common ones: 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4.

Time Signature Meaning
2/4 2 crotchet beats in a bar
3/4 3 crotchet beats in a bar
4/4 4 crotchet beats in a bar

By crotchet beats we mean beats that last for a crotchet. So a bar of 4/4 will have a total duration of 4 crotchets (or 2 minims, or 1 semibreve).

Barlines

On a score, bars are separated by barlines. Barlines are vertical lines drawn across the staff. They don't denote any sound or any gap or pause in the notes. They are simply a visual aid to separate the bars.

If we have a time signature of 4/4, then after every 4 crotchets (or equivalent note values), we must put a barline and start a new bar.

At the end of a piece we will find a final barline, which looks like two barlines next to each other, one normal and one thicker.
