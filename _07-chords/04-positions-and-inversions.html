So far, all the chords we have seen looked like this:

with the root at the bottom, the third a third above and the fifth a fifth above. But the truth is that chords can take many forms.

When we speak about a chord, like a C major chord, we are referring to the set of notes C, E and G. And C E and G can appear in many different octaves, in many different ways. All of those different ways form a C major chord as long as they contain the notes C E and G. We say that these chords are in different positions.

In fact, the concept of a chord is so broad that the notes don't have to necessarily appear vertically on the page. One of the most famous pieces for piano is the Moonlight Sonata by Beethoven. It is in C♯ minor and starts like this:

If you look at the notes in the first bar, you'll see that they form a C♯ minor chord. However they don't all appear at the same time. The E doesn't appear until the third quaver, and the three notes of the chord are never played together. Nevertheless, the sound of the notes blends together and the ear hears the C♯ minor harmony.

Generally speaking, you don't need to worry about the posistion a chord is in, as they all behave in basically the same way, but we do need to take note of which note appears lowest in the chord, (we call this appearing in the bass).

Inversions

In the examples above, the root of the chord was always in the bass. We say that those chords are in root position. When one of the other notes is the lowest we say that the chord is in an inversion.

  • When the root is in the bass, the chord is in root position.
  • When the third is in the bass, the chord is in first inversion.
  • When the fifth is in the bass, the chord is in second inversion.

Labelling

In the last section we learnt about labelling chords according to what scale degree they are. In addition to that, we will also label them with the letters a, b or c, depending on their inversion:

  • a for root position
  • b for first inversion
  • c for second inversion

Together with the roman numerals to indicate the scale degree of the chord (which we learnt about in the last section) this will be our system for labelling chords. Note that the roman numerals only make sense once we have established the key, as they indicate scale degrees in that key, so finding the key is the first step in labelling the chords. In the grade 5 exam, the key will always be given to you, but it's an important that you develop the skill of being able to identify the key of a piece or extract.
