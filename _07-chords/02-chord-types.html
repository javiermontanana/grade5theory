--- layout: chapter title: Types of Triad ---

To construct our triads we will be stacking major or minor thirds on top of each other. There are 4 combinations we can have:

  • major third then minor third
  • minor third then major third
  • two minor thirds
  • two major thirds

Each of those combinations will create a different type of triad. These types are called major, minor, augmented and diminished. These are of course the same words we use to describe intervals. The fact that we use those words can help you understand these types of chords better by relating their name to the intervals between the notes.

Let's try to form each of those types of triad on the note D.

Major Triad

If we have a major third and then a minor third on top, we have a major triad. A major triad has a perfect fifth between the root and the fifth.

Minor Triad

If we have a minor third and then a major third on top, we have a minor triad. A major triad also has a perfect fifth between the root and the fifth.

Diminished Triad

If we have a minor third and then another minor third on top, we have a diminished triad. A diminished triad has a diminished fifth between the root and the fifth.

Augmented Triad

If we have a major third and then another major third on top, we have an augmented triad. An augmented triad has an augmented fifth between the root and the fifth.

As you may have realised, the name we give each type of triad is closely related to the intervals it contains. In a diminished triad, the fifth is a diminished fifth above the root. In an augmented triad, the fifth is an augmented fifth above the root. In major and minor triads, the fifth is a perfect fifth above the root, so their name relates the third. In a major triad, the third is a major third above the root. In a minor triad, the third is a minor third above the root. As you become more familiar with chords, you'll see that major and minor triads are by far the most common. Diminished triads are less common, and augmented triads are quite rare.

Identifying Chords by their Root and Type

If we know the root and the type of a certain chord, that automatically tells us all the notes in that chord. This means we can identify chords by their root and type and that gives us the name, so to speak, of the chord. So the four chords we formed above would be called D major, D minor, D diminished and D augmented.

For exmaple, if I speak about a G major chord, that tells you all the notes in the chord. You know that there's a major third and a perfect fifth, so it contains the notes G, B and D. A G minor chord contains G B♭ and D.

Major and Minor Triads as Tonic Triads

Major and minor triads are also the triads formed on the tonic of major and minor keys. For example, the D major triad is what we obtain if we stack a third and a fifth top of the note D and we use the notes from the D major scale (so an F♯ and an A). If we use the notes from D minor, we would instead obtain a D minor triad (we would us F♮ and A).

This can be a useful way to think about major and minor triads: as tonic triads. By this I mean that if I want to work out what notes there are in, let's say, an E major chord, I will build a triad on the note E and I will use notes from the E major scale. I can use the key signature for E major or the E major scale to work out what notes I need, but I will use a G♯ and a B.

If you practise arpeggios on your instrument, what you are playing are the notes from the triad of the chord that the arpeggio is named after.
