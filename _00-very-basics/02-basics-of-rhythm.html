Bars and Time Signatures

Most music is organised into bars which have 2, 3, or 4 beats. The beat is a steady pulse that fits the music. If you clap along at a steady pulse, you will probably clap the beat. The first beat of each bar feels stronger, so music that has 2 beats in a bar feels like it's going: 1, 2, 1, 2, Music with three beats in a bar feels like it's going: 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3 And music with 4 beats in a bar feels: 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4, The time signature tells us about the number of beats in a bar. The time siganture is a pair of number that we write at the beginning of the first line of a piece. 2/4 3/4 and 4/4 are examples of time signatures. they have 2,3 and 4 beats in a bar respectively, and the beats are crtochets.
Time Signature Meaning
2/4 2 crtochet beats in a bar
3/4 3 crtochet beats in a bar
4/4 4 crtochet beats in a bar

Barlines

On a score, bars are separated by barlines. Barlines are vertical lines across the staff. They don't denote any sound or any gap in the notes or anything like that. They are just there to separate the bars, the groups of 2, 3 or 4 beats. If we have a time signature of 4/4, then after every 4 crothcets or equivalent note values, we must put a barline and start another bar. At the end of a piece we will find a final barline, which looks like two barlines next to each other, one normal and one thicker. Look at the example below: The time signature is 4/4, so each bar lasts for 4 crotchets. The second bar has 3 notes: 2 crotchets and 1 minim, but that is still equivalent to 4 crotchets. So every 4 crtochets we have a barline. In this case, the piece is 4 bars long and a the end we have a final barline.
