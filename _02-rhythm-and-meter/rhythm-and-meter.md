




# Note values


by value we mean duration 

in this chapter we are going to learn everything to do with rhythm and meter. 

If you have learnt that a crotchet is one beat, you must forget that. 
That is a useful way to start learning about note values, 
but it is not always true that a crotchet is one beat. 








## dots

A dot next to a note adds half of its value to that note. 



N.B.: Not to be confused with staccato dots. 


### double dots

A note can also be "double-dotted" meaning it has 2 dots. 
The second dot adds half of the value of the first dot. 


In principle, there could be more than two dots, 
and each dot will add half of the value of the previous dot, 
but it's unusual to see more than 2. 



## ties 

A tie connects two notes and means that they should be played as a single note 
whose value is the sum of the two. 

So for example, a crotchet tied to another crotchet would be a note that lasts for two crotchets. 

This can be very useful if we want a note to start in one bar and last until the middle of the following bar. Then we will need to use a tie. 

[example]

We will later see that ties are sometimens also used to for notes 
that start in one beat of the bar and go into the next beat. 




Ties look exactly the same as slurs, and can be easily confused. 

Slurs indicate that notes should be played in a smooth and connected way 
but don't affect the duration of the notes. 

Ties must always connect notes of the same pitch, 
whereas slurs can connect notes of different pitch.

# bars and time signatures

Music is organised into bars. 

bars are separated by barlines. 

A bar is a unit of musical time.
The way notes are grouped into bars 
follows some rules governed by the time signature. 


## time signatures 

A time signature consists of two numbers one on top of the other, 
which tell us the length of a bar in a particular piece.

The time signature is placed at the beginning, on the first staff, 
right after the clef and the key signature. 

A time signature tells us how many of what note value 
there will be in each bar 
(later we will see that the time signature can also tell us something
about how the bar is divided into beats). 

### understanding time signatures 

A time signature has two numbers, 
the top number, and the bottom nummber. 

The top number simply tells us how many are in each bar. 
This could be any number. 
if it's a 2, then there are 2 in each bar. 

The bottom number tells us of what. 
The bottom number will always be a power of 2 (either 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 etc...)
and each of these numbers corresponds to a note value, such that 
1 is a semibreve, 2 is a minim, 4 is a crotchet, 8 is quavers, etc...


Common time and cut time:
C = 4/4
cut C = 2/2




So, for example: 
2/4 would tell us that each bar is 2 crotchets long. 
3/4 3 crotchets
4/4 4 crotchets
2/2 2 minims
6/8 6 quavers
9/8 9 quavers


You may have noticed that some of the examples above are the same length as others. 
4 crotchets is equivalent to 2 minims, so a bar of 4/4 and 2/2 are the same length. 
6 quavers is equivalent to 3 crotchets, 

That's true. 
But the time signature tells us more about the meter of the music 
than just how long each bar is, so we will see that there are important differences between those. 


### types of time signatures

In the following sections, we will encounter many different time signatures, 
and we will learn what each one means. 

We can divide time signatures into 2 types: regular and irregular. 

In regular time signatures, the bars will be divided into 2, 3, or 4 beats, 
and those beats are the same length. 
There are two kinds of regualar time signatures: simple and compound. 

We will see that simple time signatures have a 2, 3 or 4 as the top number 
and compound time signatures have a 6, 9 or 12 as the top number,
but the essential difference between simple and compound time 
is that in simple time signatures, the beats are divided into 2 equal parts whereas 
in compound time signatures, the beats are divided into 3 equal parts. 


In irregular time signatures, the way the bar is divided into beats 
doesn't necessarily follow any rules 
and can change from one bar to the next, 
so we won't worry about how many beats are in a bar, 
or how long those beats are, or anyhting like that. 
Irregular time signatures are those whose top number is anything other than
2, 3, 4, 6, 9 or 12



# simple time 

Simple time signatures are those with 2, 3, or 4 as the top number. 
This top number is the number of beats in a bar. 

So the time signature tells us in a very straightforward way how many beats 
there are and how long the beats are. 

For exmaple, 
2/4 has 2 crotchets in a bar. It has 2 beats, and each beat is a crotchet. 
4/8 has 4 quavers in a bar. It has 4 beats in a bar and each beat is a quaver. 

Here we can see the difference between two time signatures which have the same bar-lenght. 
Both 2/4 and 4/8 have a bar that is a minim long (2 crothcets or 4 quavers) 
but 2/4 has 2 beats in a bar and 4/8 has 4 beats in a bar. 
This is a subtle but important difference. 

other examples: 
C 4/4
cut C 2/2

In all simple time signatures, the value of the beat will be a simple note value (not dotted), 
which can be divided into 2 subdivisions. 

eg. 4/4, the beat is a crotchet, which divides into 2 quavers. 

As we mentioned earlier this is what makes these simple time signatures. 
We will see in the next section that, in compound time signatures, 
the beat will be a dotted note value, which will be divided into 3 subdivisions. 


# compound time 

Compound time signatures are those with 6, 9, or 12 as the top nnumber,
**but remember that they still have 2, 3, or 4 beats in a bar.**

In the case of compound time signatures, 
**the top number tells us the number of subdivisions in the bar,** 
**not the number of beats.**

In coumpound time signatures, each beat has 3 subdivisions, 
so when the top number is 6, we have 2 beats in a bar,
when the top number is 9 we have 3 beats in a bar,
and when the top number is 12 we have 4 beats in a bar. 

Let's look at some examples: 

The most common compound time signatures are the ones with 8 as the bottom number: 
6/8, 9/8 and 12/8

In 6/8 we have 6 quavers in a bar. 
Remember that the quavers are not the beats, they are the subdivision of the beats. 
We have 2 beats in a bar. 
Each beat is 3 quavers long, so a dotted crotchet long. 

As we said earlier, in compound time signatures, the beats are always a dotted note value, 
which can be divided into 3 equal sibdivisions. 

In 9/8, we have 3 beats in a bar, 
again, each beat is made up of 3 quavers, so a dotted crotchet long.  

12/8 is similar: 4 beats, each 3 quavers long. 

In 6/4, 
we have 2 beats in a bars. 
Each beat is 3 crotchets long, so a dotted minim long.

# the importance of the concept of beats in a bar

A lot of what we cover in the next few sections will be to do with this.

such that the first beat is the strongest,

# writing the same rhythm in a different time signature 

In this section, we will be rewriting a certain given rhythm in a new time signature 
and with different note values. 

Here, you should remember that what essentially identifies a rhythm is the placement of notes 
on certain beats (or fractions of beats) within the bar. 
It is not about using certain note values, so we will be changing the note values, 
but the structure of the bars. 

There are 2 ways in which will do this. 
the first is by doubling or halving note values

The second will be going between simple and compound time, 
and for that we will need to use something called triplets or duplets. 


## doubling or halving note values. 

Let's take a simple example. 
Suppose we have the following rhythm:
[example] 2/4 - cro qua qua

We are going to try to write that rhythm using note values that are twice as long. 

Let's think of our new time signature first. 
In 2/4 each beat is a crotchet, so in our new time signature the beats 
will also have to be twice as long, so minims. 

We will still need two beats in a bar. 
Remember that the number of beats in a bar is essential to the rhythm, 
and because we want to write *the same rhythm* we must not change the number of 
beats in a bar. 

Our new time signatue will therefore be 2/2, as it has 2 minim beats in a bar. 

In our new time signature, if we double the note values, we will have the same rhythm as above. 

[example]2/2 min cro cro

This is the same rhythm because we have the same thing going on in each beat of the bar as before. 
In the first beat we have one note taking up the whole beat, 
and in the second beat we have one note taking up the first half,
and another taking up the second half. 

Instead of doubling all note values, we could have halved the note values. 
In that case our new time signature would be 2/8 and the rhythm would be written like this: 
[example]2/8 qua sem sem


When we double or halve note values, 
we will never be changing the top number of the time signature. 
As this would change the number of beats in the bar 
(we will change the top number later 
when we rewrite rhythms from simple time to compound and vice versa, but not here) 
we only need to change the bottom number. 
Remember that the bottom number tells us what kind of notes we are counting in, 
so we can double that and double all the note values and the rhythm remains the same. 


Let's look at and example of this in comound time. 

[example]6/8 cr q qqq

Let's try to write that rhythm doubling the note values. 

First we will need to change our time signature to 6/4.
6/4 keeps the same beats in a bar and the same structure as 6/8.

Then we double all the note values. 
[example]6/4 min cr cr cr cr


It is important to note that when we double the note values, 
we are not doubling the length or duration of the notes. 

Remember that what is essential to the rhythm is the beats, 
so we are assuming that our beat pulse is the same. 
If that is the case, then the rhythm will sound exactly the same 
whether it is written in one time signature or another. 



## going between simple and compound - triplets and duplets 

Now let's try to take a rhythm in a compound time signature 
and write it in a simple time signature. 

[example]6/8 dtcro qqq

The first thing is to change the time signature. 
6/8 has 2 beats in a bar, 
so we need a time signature that has 2 beats in a bar. 
I will use 2/4 as it keeps the note values similar, 
but any time signature with a 2 as the top number would be correct. 

Now I need to write the same rhythm. 
In the first beat, we have one note taking up the whole beat, 
so in 2/4 that will be a crotchet. 

But in the second beat, we run into a problem. 
We need 3 equal notes that divide the beat. 
This is what compound time signatures are desinged for, 
but we need to do it in a simple time signature. 

For this, we need to use something called a triplet. 

A triplet indicates that we will fit 3 notes where we would normally fit 2. 
In this case, we are 2/4 so the second beat is a crotchet, 
that which fits 2 quavers, 
and so by using a triplet we can fit 3 quavers. 

A triplet consists of a number 3 and a bracket over the notes it contains like so: 

[exmaple]3 triplet quavs

So the example above, in 2/4 would be written like this:
[example]2/4 cro triplet quavs

Now we have the same rhythm,, but written in a compound time signature. 
Again, we will assume that our beat pulse is the same, and that will mean that it sounds the same. 
So it we had crotchet = 60 bpm in 2/4, we will need to have dotted crotchet = 60 bpm in 6/8


-----
Sometimes the bracket will be omitted, so the triplet would look like this:
[example] 
Even the number 3 can be omitted, 
and in that case you need to need to guess that those notes are a triplet. 
[exmaple]
Composers and editors should try to be clear about what they are writing, 
and it is bad practice to omit information and leave things unclear, 
but there are many cases where the triplet is perfectly clear from the beaming of the quavers,
and from the broader context (say, if  the same rhythm has appeared before) 
and it can actually make it easier to read if the 3 and the bracket are omitted. 
--


The triplet means that we are fitting 3 in the space of 2. 
So if the triplet is over a duration of a crotchet, 
it means that we are going to fit 3 quavers in that crotchet 
(normally, 2 quavers fit in a crotchet).
But this doesn't mean that the triplet is going to contain 3 notes, 
which are quavers. 
Our triplet will contain note values which are equivalent to 3 quavers, 
but it can contain many different note values. 

Let's look at some examples. 

6/8 cr q dtcr

let's say we want to write this rhythm in 2/4 

In the first beat, we have a note that takes up 2 thirds of the beat, 
and one that takes up the 3rd third. 

So we need to divide our beat in 2/4 into 3. 
For this we will use a triplet again over the first crotchet of the bar, 
meaning that we can now fit 3 quavers in it.

Of those 3 quavers, now 2 will go to the first note, and one to the second note, 
so the first note will be a crotchet inside the triplet: 
[example]

Inside a triplet, a crotchet is still equal to 2 quavers! 


In the example above, you can think of a crotchet as 2 quavers tied together. 
This helps avoid any confusion 

[exmaple]6/8 tied

becomes

[exmaple] 2/4 ttied


In the next example, we will have shorter note values, so you can't use the ties to help you. 

Let's try to write the following in 2/4: 

[example] 6/8 nice cup of tea - sicilienne 

Here in the first beat, we have a note on each subdivision, 
plus a note half way through the second subdivision. 

We can achieve this in 2/4 with a triplet and 2 semiquavers in the middle bit of the triplet. 

Basically, in each case, we can keep the note values the same, 
and just stick a triplet on top, 
and that will take care of it. 

[exmaple] 2/4 nice cup of tea - sicilienne. 

In all these cases, we would call those a triplet of quavers, 
because we are fitting in the space of 2 quavers (or a crotchet) 
notes whcih have a total value of 3 quavers, 
even though the notes on the page are not 3 quavers. 


### duplets 

Just like a triplet allows us to fit 3  in the space of 2, 
a duplet allows us to fit 2 in the space of 3. 

This is going to be very useful when converting from a simple to a compound time signature. 

Let's look at an example. 

[example]2/4 cr q q 

In the second beat, we have 2 notes each taking up half the beat. 

Let's try to write that in 6/8.

For the first beat, we just need one note taking up the whole beat. 
That will be a dotted crotchet. 
For the second beat we need two notes, each taking up half the beat. 

So what we will do is put a duplet over the second beat. 
This means that now we will fit just two quavers which will take up the space of 3. 

[example]6/8 dtcr dup q q

Just like, with triplets, in a duplet of quavers, we can incude other note values. 

[example]2/4 cr q sem sem

The  example above can be written as:

[example]6/8 dtcr dup q sem sem


# other tuplets 

## more on triplets and duplets

In the previous section, we saw  examples of triplets and duplets 
where they were used to divide a single beat into 2 or 3 parts. 

But this is not the only use of triplets and duplets. 
Remember the general meaning: 
a triplet fits 3 in the space of 2.
a duplet fits 2 in the space of 3. 

We can use this definition and have triplets or duplets with any note values. 

For exmaple, a 2/2 bar is 2 crotchets long. 
I could have a triplet of crotchets, allowing me to fit 3 crotchets into a bar. 


## other tuplets

Just like triplets and duplets there are many other kinds of **tuplets** 
which allow us to fit a certain number of notes 
in the space that would normally correspond to another number.

sextuplets fit 6 in the space of 4
quadruplets fit 4 in the space of 6
quintuplets fit  5 in the space of 4
septuplets fit 7 in the space of 4


And we can keep going. 
Basically, if you want 13 or however many equal notes to fit into a crotchet, 
you can just stick a 13 with a bracket on top...

in practice you do not have to memorise the table above. 
It is useful to memorise the meaning of triplet and duplet, 
but in all other cases, you will be able to figure out how long each group takes from the context.

# irregular time signatures 

All of the time signatures we saw earlier were regular. 
These were the ones with top numbers 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12.

Time signatures with any other number as a top number are known as irregular. 

Here, the very first thing we learnt about what the time signature means still applies: 
the time signature tells us how many of what fits in each bar. 

For example, 7/4 has 7 crotchets. 5/8 has 5 quavers. 

But that is as far as we can go.
We don't have rules that tell us anything about beats in the bar. 
The whole point is that in irregular time signatures, 
the notes can be organised into beats in many different ways, 
that is why they are called irregular. 

A 5/8 bar can be 
2 quavers + 3 quavers, 
or 3 quavers + 2 quavers,
or 1 quaver + 2 quavers + 2 quavers, 
etc....
