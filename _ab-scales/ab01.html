Major Scales

the C major Scale

As we already know, the natural notes form a C major scale. That is to say that they form a series of 8 notes, with tones and semitones arranged in this way: semitones between notes 3-4 and 7-8. This is really what a major scale is, and it's important that you memorise that.

You might have noticed that the bottom half of the scale looks a lot like the top half. We have two sets of 4 notes, with the the semitones between notes 3 and 4. A set of 4 notes is called a tetrachord. One very helpful way to understand the Major scale is to see it as two tetrachord like that.

Here is the c major scale again, this time on 2 octaves, with the two tetrachords and the semitones highlighted.

Now, let's try to make a major scale starting on a different note. In principle, I could pick any note to start my new scale, but I'm going to use the fact that the two tetrachords are the same and I'm going to do it so that the top tetrachord of C major becomes the bottom tetrachord of my new scale. By doing it this way, the first four notes of my new scale are already done. Our scale will start g-a-b-c.

G is note 1 of the scale so it will be a G major scale. If we just use all natural notes, we don't have a G major scale, because we don't have the second semitone in the right place.

But it's very close! All we need to do is raise F to an F♯ and there we have it.

G major scale

Now we have all the tones and semitones in the right place. So this is a G major scale.

The process we have just carried out to get the G major scale from the C major scale, we can now repeat with the G major scale, to obtain the D major scale.

Notice that what we are doing each time is making a new scale that starts on note 5 (a perfect fifth above) of the previous scale, and adding the extra sharp we need to note 7 of the new scale (or note 4 of the old one).

We can keep doing this to form all of the following scales.

Scales with flats

In the previous section we formed a lot of different major scales, all with sharps. In this section we will form all of the major scales which have flats.

We will start again from C major, and this time we will form a new scale such that the top tetrachord of our new scale is the bottom tetrachord of C major. This is the other way round to what we did in the previous section.

C-D-E-F will be notes 5,6,7,8 of our new scale which means that our scale will be F major (note 1 and 8 are the same)

Starting on F with just natural notes we don't quite have a major scale, but it's very close! We just need to lower the B to a B♭.

Now we have all the tones and semitones in the rigth place, so we have an F major scale.

We can keep doing this to each new scale that we make: Creating a new major scale by starting on note 4 of the previous scale (or a perfect fifth below) and flattening note 4 of the new scale (or note 7 of the old scale).

If we keep repeating this we obtain all the following major scales.

B flat major

E flat major

A flat major

D flat major

G flat major

C flat major

Keys and key signatures

Keys

A piece of music that uses notes from a certain scale is said to be in the key of that scale. A piece (or part of a piece) that uses notes from the D major is said to be in the key of D major.

In this case, the music would contain a lot of F sharps and C sharps. Rather than writing a sharps whenever a C or an F comes up, we can use a key signature.

Key signatures

A key signature is a set of sharps or flats placed at the beginning of each stave, next to the clef. These sharps or flats will apply to any notes of that letter name. So, for example, if we have a piece with an F♯ in the key signature, any Fs will automatically be sharp. If an F♮ appears in the music, it will have to have the ♮ sign next to it. Each key has it's corresponding key signature.

Order of sharps and flats

If you look back at all the major scales we worked out earlier in this chapter, you can see how many sharps or flats each scale has. G major has one sharp (F♯), D major has two (F♯ and C♯), F major has one flat (B♭) and so on...

You will also see that there is an order to the sharps and flats that is never broken. F♯ is the first, C♯ is the second, and so on. By this I mean that there isn't a scale where the C is sharp but the F isn't. F♯ is the first sharp, so it is there is all the scales with one or more sharps. Same for the flats, B♭ is the first, E♭ is the second.

The order of sharps is the following: F - C - G - D - A - E - B

The order of flats is the following: B - E - A - D - G - C - F

There are a couple of things we can notice that will make it very easy to remember.

  • The order of flats is exactly the reverse to that of the sharps.
  • Each sharp is a fifth up from the last, so all you need to remember is that F is the first, and then you should be able to list all of them.
  • Since the order of flats is the reverse of the sharps, each new flat is a fifth down from the last.

how to write key signatures

- each sharp or flat only once. - in the right order, reading from left to right. - in the right octave for each clef.

When we write a key signature, it's important that the sharps or flats go on the right stave position.

There are a few conventions we should follow when writing key signatures:

  • Each sharp or flat should be written only once. The sharps and flats in the key signature apply to any notes of that letter name in any octave, so
  • The sharps or flats should be in the right order. If we read the key signature from left to right, the first sharp should be F, the second C, and so on. For flats, the first should be B, the second E and so on.
  • Each sharp or flat should be in the right octave on the stave. In each clef, there is a conventional position for each sharp or flat. For example, in treble clef, the first sharp goes on the top stave line, not on the bottom space even though both of them would be a valid F♯. The way to learn these positions is to memorise what the key signatures with 7 flats and 7 sharps look like in each clef.

These conventions are followed universally nowadays, and will be followed in most printed music that you are likely to see. However, they are not always followed in some older editions. In the grade 5 theory exam, you are expected to know and follow them.

Besides being a useful tool for writing music, key signature give us a useful short hand for describing scales. We will say things such as "D major has two sharps" by which we mean that it has 2 sharps in the key signature, which if we know the order of sharps, tells us that it has an F♯ and a C♯.

Aside: a tip that might help you identify key signatures for major scales:
- no major scale without flat in the name has any flats, except f major
- for a major scale with sharps, the last sharp in the key sign is the seventh note
- for a major scale with flats, the tonic is the second-to-last flat

Minor Scales

We have leanrt about major scales. There is another kind of scale we need to learn about: minor sclaes.

Minor scales are a bit more complicated because there are 3 forms of the minor scale that we need to learn: natural, harmonic and melodic.

A minor natural melodic and harmonic

A minor is the key with no sharps or flats in the key signature. In this section we will learn the 3 forms of A minor: natural, melodi and harmonic. In the next section we wiil learn all the other keys and their key signatures.

As we mentioned, minor scales come in 3 different forms. The natural form has the notes given by the key signature. In the melodic and harmonic forms we will need to raise some notes by a semitone with an accidental. This means that

The natural minor scale is the most straightforward. We just use the notes given by the key signature, so in the case of A minor, all the notes are natural.

For the harmonic scale, we raise note 7 by a semitone going up and going down. In this case note 7 is a G natural, so it will become G sharp.

for the melodic scale, we will raise notes 6 and 7 going up only.

All other minor scales

Now that we have learnt about A minor, we can work out all the other minor scales with all the different key signatures. These will work in a very similar way to the major scales: we will go up a perfect fifth to go up one more sharp (or one fewer flat) and we will go down a perfect fifth to go up one more flat (or one fewer sharp).

Let's do as an example, the minor key with one flat. From A we go down a perfect fifth and we get to D, so the key with one flat is D minor.

for the natural minor scale we just use the key signature, so the only note that is not natural is B♭.

For the harmonic scale, we raise note 7, so note 7 will be C♯.

for the melodic scale, we raise notes 6 and 7 going up, so note 6 is B♮ and note 7 is C♯, but going up only. So going down, note 7 is C♮ and note 6 is B♭.

relative keys

As you can see, for each key signature, there is a major key and a minor key. We call keys which have the same key signature relative. A minor is the relative minor of C major, B♭ major is the relative major of G minor, etc. The relative major is always a minor 3rd above the relative minor.
