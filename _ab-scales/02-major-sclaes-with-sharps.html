Now let's try to make a major scale starting on a different note. I could pick any note to start my new scale, but I'm going to use the fact that the two tetrachords are the same and I'm going to do it so that the upper tetrachord of C major (G-A-B-C) becomes the lower tetrachord of my new scale. By doing it this way, the first four notes of my new scale are already done.

G is note 1 of our new scale so it will be a G major scale. Let's see what happens if we just use natural notes. We would have this scale:

This is not a G major scale, but it's very close! The second semitone is in the wrong place (between notes 6 and 7). All we need to do is raise the F to an F♯ and there we have it:

G major scale

Now we have all the tones and semitones in the right place. So this is a G major scale.

Here is a visualisation of what we've just done, We'll start with the C major scale in 2 octaves, then we start our new scale from G but still use all the notes from C major, then we just raise the F to and F♯ and we have G major.

We can now repeat this process, starting with G major, obtain the D major scale.

Notice that what we are doing each time is making a new scale that starts on note 5 (a perfect fifth above) of the previous scale, and adding the extra sharp we need to note 7 of the new scale (or note 4 of the old one).

We can keep doing this to each new scale, and we obtain all of the following scales.
