--- layout: chapter title: Scales 1 - The C Major Scale ---

As we know, the natural notes form a C major scale. In the natural notes, there are semitones between E and F and between B and C, and all the other steps are tones. This means that there are semitones between notes 3 and 4 and between notes 7 and 8.

What you must understand before going on to learn about all the other major scales is that this fact—the fact that there are semitones in notes 3-4 and 7-8 and the other steps are tones—is what defines the major scale. A major scale is a scale that has that particular arrangement of tones and semitones.

In the next few sections of this chapter, we will form all the other major scales. This means that we will start on a different note and from that note we will form a scale that has this same pattern of tones and semitones. To do this, we will need to use sharps and flats on certain notes. C major is the only major scale that uses only natural notes.

Tetrachords

You might have noticed that the bottom half of the scale looks a lot like the top half. If we split the scale in half, we have two sets of 4 notes with the semitones between notes 3 and 4. A set of 4 notes is called a tetrachord, and it is going to be very helpful to think of the major scale as consisting of two tetrachords with the pattern: Tone-Tone-Semitone.

The upper tetrachord starts on note 5 (G in this case) which is a tone above note 4. It is also a perfect fifth above note 1. Remember that all these properties we are noticing about C major will be true of any major scale.
