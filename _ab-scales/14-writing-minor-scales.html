--- layout: chapter title: Scales 13 - Writing minor scales (without a key signature) ---

Earlier in this chapter, we learnt to write any major scale without a key signature. We did this by learning the pattern of semitones using it to work out all the notes in the scale. In this section we will do a similar thing for minor scales.

There are many ways of doing this, but I will show you one where we divide the scale in two parts.

  • The bottom 5 notes (from the tonic up to the dominant)
  • The top 4 notes (from the dominant up to the tonic)

The bottom 5 notes

The first 5 notes of any minor scale look like this:

With a semitone between notes 2 and 3, with the other steps as tones. You can think of this as a major scale with the third degree lowered.

A major

A minor

The top 4 notes

The second part of the minor scales is more complicated, as it is the part that differs between the 3 forms of the scale. In the melodic scale it also differs between the ascceding and descending form.

It is worth remembering that the dominant is always a perfect fifth above the tonic, I will use A minor in these examples, so we will always start from the note E.

The scale degrees we have to fill in are 6 and 7.

The natural minor

In the natural minor, we just use the notes form the key signature. In a minor there are no sharps and flats in the key signature, so the notes e-f-g-a give us the pattern of tones and semintones.

In the natural minor scale we have a semitone between scale degrees 5 and 6, the rest tones.

The harmonic scale

In the harmonic minor scale we raise the leading note, so we have a semitone between 5 and 6, a semitone between 7 and 8, and an augmeented second between scale degrees 6 and 7.

The melodic minor scale

In the melodic minor, going up we have a semitone between the leading note and the tonic, and the rest tones. This is exactly like the second tetrachord of the major scale. Maybe you had already realised this, but the melodic minor scale going up is just like the major scale but with the lowered third degree.

Going down, the melodic is like the natural minor.

Let's practise

If this is all new to you, it may seem like a lot to memorise, but with practice I promise you it can become very easy.

As an example, let's try to write down the scales of B major and B minor in all the forms, without using key signatures.

the first thing I will do is identify the tonic and the dominant. The tonic is of course B. The dominant is a perfect fifth above, so it is F♯ in all the scales.

B major

For B major we will fill in with the two tetrachords, the first starting from the tonic, the second starting from the dominant.

B minor

For B minor, we will need to lower the 3rd degree and that gives us the first half of the scale.

Now we will fill the second half of the scale for each of the forms of the minor.

Natural minor

In the natural minor we need a semitone between 5 and 6 and the rest tones, so we fill in with G natural and A natural

harmonic minor

for the harmonic minor, we need a semitone between degrees 5 and 6, and a semitone between the leading note and the tonic. This gives us the augmented second between 6 and 7.

Melodic minor

For the melodic minor ascending form, we need the semitone between the leading note and the tonic, but we also need tones for the rest (remember that we are aoiding the augmented second). We could also just take the second tetrachord from the B major scale.

for the descending form, we want a semitone between degrees 5 and 6, and tones for the rest, just like in the natural minor.
