--- layout: chapter title: Scales 12 - Why are these notes raised in the minor scales? ---

In the previous section, we discussed how the tonic is the final arrival point of the music. This is achieved through the relationship between the dominant and the tonic. In particular, the dominant chord has need of resolution towards the tonic chord. You'll learn more about chords in a different chapter, but very briefly, the dominant chord is a set of notes which includes the 7th scale degree, and in order for the chord to function as a dominant, the 7th must be raised in minor keys. That is to say, the semitone between the leading note and the tonic is essential for establishing the tonic as the key.

For this reason, the natural minor scale is not often used. It doesn't contain the raised leading note, so it does not by itself establish a key. The melodic and harmonic scales are the more commonly used ones, they are probably the ones you play on your instrument, required in exams. In fact, the natural minor scale doesn't appear in the grade 5 theory exam.

The need for the raised leading note explains why we raise the 7th scale degree in the harmonic scale. What about the melodic scale, then?

Melodic comes from the word melody. A melody is a sequence of notes, a tune. to do with how a line of notes moves up or down. In order to write melodies that have melodic fluency, we try to follow certain rules. One of these rules is avoiding augmented intervals.

So let's say we have a melody that goes up the scale, from the dominant to the tonic.

We want the semitone between the leading note and the tonic, but if we just raise the leading note like in the harmonic scale, we have an augmented 2nd between scale degrees 6 and 7. In order to avoid this augmented 2nd, we also raise scale degree 6. And that's how we obtain that part of the melodic scale.

But why is it different when we go the other way? When we move down from the tonic to the dominant,

we don't need to raise the leading note at all because we are moving away from the tonic. The leading note wants to resolve up to the tonic and in the case where we go down the scale we are doing precisely the opposite, so in this case, the melodic motion downwards is facilitated by not raising the 7th degree. As we are not raising the 7th degree, we don't need to raise the 6th either, so we just use the same notes as in the natural scale.

Just like melodic has to do with melody, harmony has to do with harmony. Harmony is to do with chords, which you will learn about in a different chapter. This is perhaps not so helpful for telling us what the harmonic scale is (especially if you haven't learnt about chords and cademnces yet) but the logic behind this is that in order for the dominant chord to fulfill it's function, we need to raise the leading note (this is also to do with creating a pull towards the tonic.)

This explanation for the melodic and harmonic scales is meant to help you remember and understand them better. But you shouldn't take these as hard rules that mean that the melodic scale must be used for melodies or anything like that. In practice, composers use scales rather freely.

The reality is that in music that is in minor keys, the leading note is very often raised, and the 6th degree is also sometimes raised, and it is often following the principles outlined above.
