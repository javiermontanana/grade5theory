why those notes raised in the minor scales

In our previous discussion of keys, we saw how the leading note is essential for establishing a key. To recap, in order to establish the tonic as the final arrival point of the music, we need the semitone between scale degree 7 (the leading note) and the tonic.

For this reason, the natural minor scale is not often used. It doesn't contain the raised leading note, so it does not by itself establish a key.

The melodic and harmonic scales are the more commonly used ones, they are probably the ones you play on your instrument, required in exams. In fact, the natural minor scale doesn't appear in the grade 5 theory exam.

The need for the raised leading note explains why we raise the 7th scale degree in the harmonic scale.

this fact alone can explain the harmonic scale.

What about the melodic scale, then?

Melodic comes from the word melody. A melody is a sequence of notes, a tune. to do with how a line of notes moves up or down. In order to write melodies that have melodic fluency, we try to follow certain rules. One of these rules is avoiding augmented intervals.

So let's say we have a melody that goes up the scale, from the dominant to the tonic.

We want the semitone between the leading note and the tonic, but if we just raise the leading note like in the harmonic scale, we have an augmented 2nd between scale degrees 6 and 7. In order to avoid this augmented 2nd, we also raise scale degree 6. And that's how we obtain that part of the melodic scale.

But why is it different when we go the other way? When we move down from the tonic to the dominant.

We don't actually need to raise the leading note at all because we are not creating motion towards the tonic.

In the melodic scale, when we move up from the dominant to the tonic, we want the leading note raised, but we also want to avoid an augmented interval (the augmented second between 6 and 7) so we raise note 6 as well.

When we move down from the tonic to the dominant we don't need to create this pull towards the tonic, since we are going away from it, so we dont raise the 7th scale degree. We therefore don't also need to raise the 6th either.

Just like melodic has to do with melody, harmony has to do with harmony. Harmony is to do with chords, which you will learn about in a different chapter. This is perhaps not so helpful for telling us what the harmonic scale is (especially if you haven't leartn about chords and cademnces yet) but the logic behind this is that in order for the dominant chord to fulfill it's function, we need to raise the leading note (this is also to do with creating a pull towards the tonic.)

This explanation for the construction of the melodic and harmonic scale is meant to help you remember them. But this does not mean that composers always use the melodic scale when writing melodies, or the harmonic scale only for chords. In practice, all forms of the scale may be used. But it is essential to have the raised leading note somewhere in order to establish a minor key.
